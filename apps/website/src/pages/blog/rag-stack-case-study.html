Choosing a RAG stack for hard applications

Lalit Shukla
11 Nov 2026 · Case study
RAGAgentic RAGEmbeddingsRetrieval

There is no best RAG pipeline, only the best one for a set of documents, questions and people. This case study shows how we choose each part of Smriti for six hard applications: how we chunk, which embeddings we use and why, how we retrieve, rerank and route to agents. For each choice we give what the published research supports and what we measured ourselves.

The examples come from Smriti's demo library: a synthetic railway engineering office with contracts, correction slips, tender notices, circulars in Hindi and English, a land schedule spreadsheet, scanned letters and restricted vigilance files. Every number in the measured section can be reproduced with the open-source benchmark that ships with Smriti.

Six applications, six retrieval problems

Most RAG advice assumes one kind of question over one kind of document. Real offices have several, and each breaks a different part of the pipeline.

ApplicationWhat makes it hardThe part that breaks first
Contract and tender deskAnswers turn on an exact clause, number or date; later correction slips change earlier clausesChunking that splits clauses; embeddings that blur "21 days" and "30 days"
Multilingual policy helpdeskQuestions in Hindi, documents in English, or the reverse; mixed scripts and transliterationMonolingual embedders and English-only keyword search
Scanned and visual documentsLetters, drawings, forms and tables where the layout carries the meaningText extraction, before retrieval even starts
Spreadsheets and registersAnswers are aggregations ("total area in village Rampur"), not passagesTreating rows as prose; the model doing arithmetic
Investigations and chronologiesThe answer is spread across many documents and dates; some questions are about the whole collectionTop-k retrieval, which finds passages but not patterns
RTI and reply draftingSeveral sub-questions, each needing its own search, a legal deadline and exemptions to flagSingle-shot RAG; it needs planning and tools

Across all six there is a seventh requirement that is not negotiable: a person must never retrieve a passage from a document they are not allowed to open. That decides where filtering happens (inside the search, not after it) and rules out some techniques, which we point out as we go.

Chunking: the decision that caps everything after it

A retriever can only return what the chunker made. If a clause is split across two chunks, no embedding model or reranker will put it back together. These are the main families.

TechniqueHow it worksGood forWatch out for
Fixed-size with overlapEvery N tokens, overlapping by 10 to 20%Uniform prose; a strong baselineSplits clauses, tables and lists mid-way
RecursiveSplit on headings, then paragraphs, then sentences, until under the limitMost documents; the practical defaultStill blind to meaning inside a long section
Structure-awareUse the document's own units: clause, section, article, row, slide, emailContracts, laws, policies, spreadsheetsNeeds a parser that understands the format
SemanticSplit where the embedding similarity between neighbouring sentences dropsTranscripts and unstructured notesCosts an embedding pass; gains are inconsistent (below)
Contextual headersPrefix each chunk with its document title, section path and date, or a generated summary of where it sitsAny chunk that is ambiguous on its own ("the period shall be 21 days")A generated summary costs an LLM call per chunk at ingestion
Late chunkingEmbed the whole document with a long-context model, then pool token vectors per chunkChunks that depend on earlier contextNeeds a long-context embedder; modest gains
Parent-child (small to big)Search small chunks, return their larger parent section to the modelPrecise matching with enough context to answerLarger prompts, so more prefill time
PropositionsAn LLM rewrites text into atomic, self-contained factsFact lookup over dense proseExpensive ingestion; the rewriting can introduce errors
Page imagesNo text chunks: embed each page image (see ColPali below)Visually rich pages, scans, slidesLarger index; harder to cite a sentence

The research is more sobering than the vendor blogs. A NAACL 2025 Findings study from Vectara compared semantic and fixed-size chunking on document retrieval, evidence retrieval and answer generation. It found the gains from semantic chunking were not consistent enough to justify its cost, and fixed-size chunking often did as well or better. Late chunking (Jina, 2024) gave small, consistent improvements on long-document retrieval benchmarks. The largest gain came from adding context to the chunk: Anthropic reported that contextual embeddings plus contextual BM25 cut failed top-20 retrievals by 49%, from 5.7% to 2.9%, and by 67% (to 1.9%) with a reranker added.

What Smriti does

Structure-aware chunking first: one chunk per numbered clause in contracts and codes, paragraph groups in circulars and letters, and for tables the whole table plus row groups that repeat the column names, with a structured copy kept for exact arithmetic. Anything still too long is split by sentence with one sentence of overlap. Every chunk is embedded with a short contextual header (title, reference number, date and section path), built from metadata at no LLM cost. Whether a rule has been superseded is metadata the search filters on, not text in the chunk.

Embeddings, every kind

"Embedding" covers several different representations with different strengths. A production stack usually combines two or three.

TypeExamplesStrengthWeakness
Lexical sparse (BM25)Postgres full text, Qdrant sparse, ElasticsearchExact terms: clause numbers, codes, names, amounts. Robust on new domainsNo synonyms, no cross-language matching
Dense single-vector (bi-encoder)Qwen3-Embedding, BGE-M3, multilingual-e5, Nomic, JinaMeaning, paraphrase and cross-language matching; one fast vector searchBlurs exact numbers and rare identifiers
Learned sparseSPLADE, BGE-M3 sparse outputTerm weights with learned expansion; still an inverted indexMostly English-trained; larger index than BM25
Multi-vector, late interactionColBERTv2, Jina-ColBERT, BGE-M3 multi-vectorToken-level matching; strong quality, good for rerankingOne vector per token: much more storage
Multimodal page embeddingsColPali, ColQwenRetrieves scanned and visual pages without OCRStorage per page; answers still need text to cite
Matryoshka (variable size)Qwen3-Embedding (32 to 4,096 dims), Nomic, OpenAI v3Truncate vectors to trade a little quality for memoryOnly for models trained that way
Quantized vectorsint8 (4× smaller), binary (32× smaller)Fit large collections in RAMNeeds rescoring with full vectors to keep quality

A few results shape how we use these. BEIR (NeurIPS 2021) showed that BM25 is a hard baseline to beat on new domains, and that dense models trained on one domain often fall behind it on another. ColBERT and ColBERTv2 showed that late interaction recovers much of a cross-encoder's quality at search time; ColBERTv2's residual compression cut its storage substantially. ColPali (ICLR 2025) embedded page images directly and beat text pipelines on visually rich documents, including pipelines that captioned every figure with a strong vision model. BGE-M3 produces dense, sparse and multi-vector outputs from one model across more than 100 languages. On quantization, int8 vectors with rescoring were lossless in Microsoft's Azure evaluation at about 75% less vector storage; binary vectors need a rescoring step to stay close.

Choosing an embedding model

The MTEB leaderboard (and its multilingual successor, MMTEB) is the place to start, and the wrong place to finish. Averages hide what matters for one deployment. We shortlist on six questions, in this order:

  1. Languages. Hindi, Tamil or Bengali questions over English documents need a model trained for cross-lingual retrieval, not just multilingual text. Check the retrieval tasks in your languages, not the overall average.
  2. Licence and weights. For an air-gapped install the weights must be downloadable and the licence must allow your use. Qwen3-Embedding, BGE-M3 and multilingual-e5 are open-weight and permissively licensed; check each model card.
  3. Memory and speed. The embedder shares a GPU with the LLM and reranker. A 0.6B model at FP16 needs about 1.2 GB for weights; an 8B model needs about 16 GB. Re-indexing a million chunks is a real job; measure it (below).
  4. Context length. Long chunks, parent sections and late chunking need 8k tokens or more.
  5. Instructions and prefixes. e5 needs "query:" and "passage:" prefixes; Qwen3-Embedding expects an instruction on queries. Getting this wrong costs more than choosing a slightly weaker model.
  6. Your golden set. The last word goes to recall@10 and MRR on your own questions, asked by your own users, with the permission filters on.

The Qwen3 Embedding family (0.6B, 4B and 8B, Apache-2.0) is our default: the 8B model reported first place on the MMTEB multilingual leaderboard at release (70.58, June 2025), and the 0.6B model fits beside a 4B LLM on a 12 GB GPU. For CPU-only installs we use multilingual-e5-small. Both are swappable in one line of configuration, and the benchmark below is how we decide when to swap.

Retrieval techniques

Retrieval is everything between the question and the reranker. These are the techniques worth knowing, grouped by the problem they solve.

Combining signals

  • Hybrid search with reciprocal rank fusion. Run BM25 and dense search, then add 1/(k + rank) for each list a passage appears in (Cormack, Clarke and Büttcher, SIGIR 2009, with k = 60). It needs no score calibration and rarely does worse than either list. Weighted score fusion can rank better but must be tuned per collection.
  • Filtering inside the search. Permissions, document type, date ranges and "current version only" are applied as filters in the index query, so the top-k is filled with passages the person may read. Filtering after retrieval returns fewer or no results and, worse, leaks timing and counts.

Fixing the question

  • Query rewriting. Expand abbreviations ("PG" to "performance guarantee"), resolve follow-ups ("and for works above that?") and translate. Cheap and effective.
  • HyDE (Gao et al., ACL 2023). Generate a hypothetical answer and embed that instead of the question. It helps on zero-shot dense retrieval, but a wrong detail in the fake answer steers the search, so it is a probe, never evidence.
  • Multi-query and RAG-Fusion. Ask several rephrasings and fuse their results with RRF. Raises recall on vague questions at the cost of more searches.
  • Decomposition. Split a compound question into sub-questions, retrieve for each, then combine. This is where single-shot RAG starts turning into agentic RAG.

Time and versions

  • As-of retrieval and supersession. In regulated work the right answer is the rule in force on a date. Correction slips and new circulars must mark what they replace, and retrieval must prefer the current version while still being able to answer "what was the rule in 2023?". No embedding model solves this; it is metadata.

Structure across documents

  • GraphRAG (Microsoft, 2024) extracts an entity graph, clusters it into communities and summarizes each, so it can answer whole-collection questions ("what are the main themes in these audit reports?") that top-k retrieval cannot. Indexing is expensive.
  • LightRAG (2024) keeps a graph with dual-level retrieval and cheaper updates. RAPTOR (ICLR 2024) builds a tree of recursive summaries for questions at different levels of detail.
  • Graph and summary indexes have a permission problem: a community summary mixes facts from documents with different access. Smriti does not build a cross-document graph or summary index today for this reason; any that are added must be built per access scope.

Placing the context

  • Order matters. "Lost in the middle" (TACL 2024) showed models use information at the start and end of a long context better than the middle. Send fewer, better passages, strongest first.

Reranking: precision, not recall

A reranker reads the question and each candidate together and scores how well the candidate answers it. It can only reorder what the first stage found; it cannot recover a passage the retriever missed. So the first stage is tuned for recall and the reranker for precision.

RerankerCostWhen
Cross-encoder (bge-reranker-v2-m3, Qwen3-Reranker, mxbai)One forward pass per candidate; linear in candidatesThe default. Rerank 30 to 50 candidates, send the top 5 to 8
Late interaction (ColBERT) as rerankerCheap per candidate once token vectors existWhen you already store multi-vector embeddings
LLM listwise rerankingAn LLM call over all candidatesSmall candidate sets where reasoning matters more than latency
Rules and metadata boostsFreePrefer current versions, exact clause matches, the asker's own division

Reranking is also what lets you send less to the LLM. Five well-chosen passages produce a faster prefill and a better answer than fifteen loosely relevant ones. That makes the reranker one of the biggest levers on inference cost, not just quality.

Agentic RAG: when one search is not enough

Classic RAG retrieves once and answers. Agentic RAG lets the model decide whether to retrieve, what to search for next, which tool to use and when it has enough. The research has converged on a few patterns.

  • Retrieve, reason, retrieve (ReAct, ICLR 2023). The model alternates thinking and tool calls. It handles multi-hop questions: find the tender, then its value, then the officer whose powers cover that value.
  • Self-RAG (ICLR 2024) trains the model to decide when to retrieve and to critique its own output for support and relevance.
  • Corrective RAG (2024) grades the retrieved passages and, when they are weak, rewrites the query or searches elsewhere before answering.
  • Adaptive-RAG (NAACL 2024) classifies question complexity and routes each to no retrieval, single-step or multi-step retrieval, so easy questions stay cheap.

The surveys (Singh et al., 2025) are clear that these patterns raise accuracy on complex questions and raise cost and latency with them. An agent that makes six searches and three LLM calls costs several times a single-shot answer. So routing is the most important agentic feature: in Smriti, lookup questions go to single-shot retrieval (often answered extractively, without the LLM at all), and only compound, comparative or drafting tasks go to an agent.

The security rule for agents

An agent's tools run with the permissions of the person asking, never the agent's own. Every search an agent makes is filtered exactly like a chat search, retrieved text is treated as untrusted data (it may contain injected instructions), and actions that change anything go through approval. See agents act as the asker.

What we measured

We ran Smriti's benchmark on a laptop: an AMD Ryzen 7 7435HS (16 threads) with Docker given 12 GB of memory, and both embedding servers on CPU (Hugging Face Text Embeddings Inference 1.9). The library is the synthetic demo: 14 documents in English and Hindi, 39 chunks. These numbers show the method and the size of the trade-offs, not production capacity.

1. Embedding speed: the hidden cost of a bigger model

Embedder on CPU, 350-token chunksBatch 1Batch 8Batch 321M chunks, best case
multilingual-e5-small (118M, 384 dims)3,714 tok/s5,068 tok/s5,552 tok/sabout 24 hours
Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B (1,024 dims)122 tok/s72 tok/s66 tok/sabout 56 days

The small model was 30 to 85 times faster, and the larger model got slower with bigger batches on this CPU backend. A model that is fine for embedding one question at a time can be unusable for re-indexing a library. That is why Smriti's CPU and 6 GB profiles use e5-small and keep Qwen3-Embedding for GPU profiles.

2. The golden set could not tell the models apart

Every combination passed, including a hashing embedder with no neural model at all. The golden questions use the documents' own words (clause numbers, tender numbers, "performance guarantee"), and BM25 plus the contextual headers finds them on its own. That is good news for the product and bad news for model selection: a saturated test cannot choose a model.

3. Hard questions separate them

So we wrote ten harder questions that avoid the documents' wording: paraphrases ("how long does a firm get to hand in its bank guarantee after it wins the work?") and cross-language questions (Hindi questions about English documents, an English question about a Hindi circular). We scored each setup on the same 39 chunks, fusing the lists the way Smriti does (top 20 of each, reciprocal rank fusion with k = 60).

Setup, 10 hard questionsRight document firstIn top 5MRRCross-language, top 5
BM25 only450.470 of 4
multilingual-e5-small, dense only9100.934 of 4
multilingual-e5-small + BM25 (RRF)790.813 of 4
Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B, dense only7100.794 of 4
Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B + BM25 (RRF)680.732 of 4

Four lessons, with the caveat that ten questions over 39 chunks is a small sample:

  • Keyword search cannot cross languages. BM25 found none of the cross-language answers; both dense models found all four.
  • Hybrid is not free. Equal-weight fusion helps when the question shares the document's words (the golden set) and hurts when it does not: BM25 adds confident-looking noise. The fix is not to drop BM25, which wins on clause numbers and codes, but to put a cross-encoder after fusion, weight the lists by query type, or skip BM25 when the question and documents are in different scripts. That is our next measurement.
  • Bigger was not better here. The 118M-parameter model beat the 0.6B model on first-place hits at a fraction of the compute. On a sample this small the difference is not significant; the point is that the leaderboard ranking did not carry over, so it has to be measured on your own data.
  • Measure the model and its prefixes together. Both models need the right query instructions ("query:" for e5, an instruction for Qwen3). The first version of our own benchmark silently dropped the Qwen3 instruction; a test now guards it.

4. The LLM on a 6 GB laptop GPU

The same tool sweeps an LLM server across 1, 2, 4 and 8 simultaneous users and reports time to first token, time per token, throughput and goodput against a latency target. We ran Qwen3-4B (4-bit Q4_K_M) on llama.cpp on the laptop's RTX 4050 with 6 GB of memory, with RAG-shaped prompts of about 1,450 tokens and answers of up to 256. The target: first token within 2 seconds and under 100 ms per token after that, for 90% of requests.

ConfigurationMemoryOne user8 users, totalUsers within target
Qwen3-4B, 8-bit KV cache4.6 GB53 tok/s104 tok/s2
Qwen3-4B, 16-bit KV cache5.6 GB57 tok/s83 tok/s2
Qwen3-4B, 8-bit KV, 1,000-token prompts4.7 GB51 tok/s113 tok/s4
Qwen3-4B, 8-bit KV, shared prompt prefix4.7 GB54 tok/s163 tok/s8
Qwen3-1.7B (8-bit), 8-bit KV cache3.9 GB76 tok/s110 tok/s8

This ties back to retrieval. On a small GPU the limit was not generating the answer but reading the prompt: every new 1,450-token prompt paused everyone else's answer for about 0.7 seconds. Two changes fixed most of it, and both are retrieval decisions. Sending 1,000 tokens of better-chosen passages instead of 1,450 doubled the users served within target. Keeping the start of the prompt identical across requests, so the server reuses its cached work, took it to 8 users and cut the first token to 27 ms for a single user. A smaller model was faster for one person but no better under load. Better reranking is how you send fewer tokens, which makes it an inference optimization as much as a quality one.

Smriti's 6 GB profile now uses these settings: llama.cpp with the 4-bit model, an 8-bit KV cache, 1,000 tokens of context and 4 concurrent answers. Larger cards run vLLM; we will publish those numbers with the commands to reproduce them.

The selection method

Every choice above comes from the same loop, and Smriti ships the tools for each step.

  1. Write the golden set first. Real questions, the person asking, the date, the documents that should be found and the ones that must not. Include questions that should be refused. Smriti packs keep it in evals/golden.yaml.
  2. Measure retrieval alone. Recall@10 and MRR with the LLM switched off. If the right passage is not retrieved, no model can answer from it. smriti modelbench retrieval runs every embedder and reranker combination.
  3. Then measure answers. Faithfulness, citation precision and correct refusals; RAGAS-style metrics help, but we check citations and numbers with code, not only with a judge model.
  4. Gate on security. Zero leaks on the permission suite and a passing red team, on every change. A better model that leaks is not better.
  5. Measure cost last, not never. Embedding throughput for re-indexing, reranker latency per candidate count, and LLM goodput at your latency target, with smriti modelbench embed, rerank and chat.

The tools are open source in the Smriti repository, with a tuning guide that lists every serving knob in the order we change them.
